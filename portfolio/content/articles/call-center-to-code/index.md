## Problem Statement

Software for claims work has two audiences. One is the person filing the claim. The other is the agent on the phone or at the desk, working through a queue of cases with a screen full of tools that someone else designed.

Engineers rarely sit in that second chair. We see tickets, requirements, and mockups. We don't see the agent juggling a frustrated caller, three open tabs, and a field that won't take the value it needs.

## Solution Overview

I got to sit in that chair. Early in the pandemic, unemployment claims surged, and I was moved off security work onto an unemployment claims call center. Years later, I was building an agent-facing portal for a different kind of claims work, and the call center turned out to be the best preparation I had.

### Twenty-Five Accounts a Day

On the line, I worked through more than 25 claimant accounts a day. Every one was a person waiting on money they needed, and every minute I spent fighting the tools was a minute they spent waiting. You learn fast which parts of a system help you and which parts get in your way.

### Training the Next Agents

I went on to manage and train 15 agents. Teaching someone else a workflow shows you every place it doesn't make sense. Some steps needed a paragraph of explanation, and that was usually a sign the process needed fixing more than the agent needed training. I worked on process improvements for how we handled claims, so the next round of agents had less to work around.

### Back to the Code

When I later built a portal for agents handling claims, I recognized the work. I knew what it meant to have a caller waiting while a page loaded, and how a confusing form multiplied across a full day of cases. That shaped what I paid attention to: the steps an agent repeats all day, the information they need in front of them at once, and the errors that should explain themselves.

### Outcome

The call center wasn't on any career plan, and it was some of the most useful time I've spent as an engineer. It gave me a real picture of the people who use the software I build.

Spend time with the people who use what you build, even for a day. You'll make different decisions once you know them.
